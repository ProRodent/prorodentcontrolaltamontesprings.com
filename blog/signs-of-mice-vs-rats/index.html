Mice or Rats? How to Tell the Difference in an Altamonte Springs Home

Scratching in the attic or droppings in the pantry: here is how to tell whether an Altamonte Springs home has mice or rats, and why it matters.

The first question we ask on almost every call is simple: is the activity happening up high or down low? The answer usually points toward either a roof rat or a house mouse before a technician ever sets foot on the property, and it shapes everything about how the visit is planned.

Droppings tell most of the story.

Mouse droppings are small, about the size of a grain of rice, with pointed ends, and they show up scattered wherever mice have been feeding: pantries, under sinks, inside cabinets with plumbing running through them. Rat droppings are noticeably larger, closer to the size of a raisin, and tend to cluster along the same beam or joist repeatedly rather than scatter.

If you are finding droppings in a kitchen cabinet or along a baseboard, that points toward mice. If you are finding them in the attic along a specific rafter, that points toward rats, specifically roof rats in this part of Florida.

Sound and timing.

Mice tend to be heard at floor level or inside walls, a light scratching or scurrying sound. Roof rats are heard overhead, in the attic or ceiling, often described as thumping or heavier scratching since they are larger animals moving around a confined space. Both are more active at night, which is when most homeowners first notice anything.

Where each one travels.

House mice stay close to food and shelter, favoring kitchens, pantries and cluttered garages. Roof rats favor height: attics, upper walls, and anywhere a tree limb or power line gives them a route onto the roof. If your activity is concentrated near the roofline or you have mature trees close to the house, roof rats are the more likely explanation.

Why the distinction changes the plan.

Mouse-sized gaps are about a quarter inch; rat-sized gaps are closer to half an inch. Sealing materials and trap placement both depend on knowing which animal you actually have, which is part of why a real inspection matters more than a guess from a home-improvement store display.

When you are not sure.

What to note before calling.

  • Where exactly you have seen droppings or heard activity.
  • Whether the sound is overhead or at floor level.
  • Any tree limbs touching the roof or visible gnaw marks.

You do not need to diagnose it perfectly yourself. Describing what you have seen or heard on the call is usually enough for us to have a good guess before the technician even arrives, and the inspection confirms it either way.

A quick field test if you are still unsure.

If you find a dropping and want a rough sense of what left it before calling, compare it mentally to a grain of rice versus a raisin. It is not a perfect test, droppings vary in size somewhat, but it is a reasonable first read that most homeowners can do without touching anything directly.

Combine that with where you found it. A rice-grain dropping in a kitchen cabinet is almost certainly a mouse. A larger dropping along an attic beam, especially paired with overhead scratching at night, points toward a roof rat, the more common attic animal in this part of Central Florida.

Either way, do not pick up or handle the dropping directly; note the location and let the technician assess it properly during the inspection.

Gnaw marks themselves carry a size clue too. Mice tend to leave finer, more delicate gnawing on softer materials like cardboard or insulation, while rats leave coarser, deeper gnaw marks, sometimes visible on wood trim or even softer metal ducting, consistent with their larger jaw and body size.

If you still cannot tell after checking droppings, location and sound, that uncertainty itself is a normal part of a lot of calls we take, and it is exactly what the inspection is for; you do not need a confident diagnosis before calling.

A strong, persistent ammonia-like smell concentrated in one small area, rather than spread generally, can also be a useful clue on its own, since rats in particular tend to use consistent urination spots along their regular travel routes.

Finally, consider the time of year. Roof rat activity in particular tends to climb in the fall as discussed in our seasonal prevention guide, so increased overhead activity during that stretch leans the odds slightly further toward rats rather than mice, though it is not a certainty on its own.

What to do, and not do, while you wait

These steps follow CDC cleanup guidance for rodent droppings and nests. They are general safety practice for any household.

  • Never dry sweep or vacuum droppings, urine stains or nesting material.
  • Ventilate a shut-up space first. Half an hour of airing out is the CDC's starting point.
  • Gloves on first, handwashing last. Do both every time.
  • Spray the area with disinfectant, wait roughly five minutes, then wipe it up with paper towels instead of dry material.
  • Seal used paper towels and any dead rodent in a bag before disposal. Keep bare skin out of it.
  • If you develop fever, muscle aches or trouble breathing in the weeks after cleaning up rodent droppings, tell a doctor about the exposure. We are not medical professionals and cannot judge individual health risk.

Common questions

Can I have both mice and rats at once?

Yes, it happens, especially in a larger home with varied entry points. The inspection accounts for both possibilities rather than assuming only one.

Do mice and rats use the same traps?

No, trap size and placement differ by species; a rat-sized trap is often too large to reliably catch a mouse and vice versa.

Is one species more dangerous than the other?

Both carry similar general risks from droppings and chewed materials. Neither is inherently more dangerous to people than the other.
