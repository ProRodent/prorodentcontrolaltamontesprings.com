Hearing scratching or scurrying in the attic after dark? Use this checklist before calling to speed up the visit.

Attic noises at night are one of the most common reasons Altamonte Springs homeowners call us, and they are also one of the easiest problems to describe clearly if you know what to listen and look for before you pick up the phone.

Where is the sound coming from.

Try to pin down which part of the house the sound is in: directly overhead in a specific bedroom, over the garage, near a particular soffit vent, or more generally spread across the attic. That location is often the single most useful detail for planning where the technician starts looking.

What time of night.

Rodents are mostly nocturnal, so activity in the evening and overnight hours is expected. If you are hearing it during the day too, that can suggest a larger, more established population, since daytime activity usually means the animals are not finding enough quiet time at night alone.

What kind of sound.

  • A light scratching or scurrying, often mice or a smaller rat moving along a joist.
  • A heavier thumping or dragging sound, often a larger rat or nesting activity.
  • Scratching concentrated near one spot versus moving around the attic.

Check what you can safely see.

If you have safe, easy attic access, a quick daytime look (never at night, and never if access is difficult or unsafe) can reveal droppings, gnawed material or disturbed insulation without disturbing anything. Do not attempt this if the attic is hard to reach or poorly lit; leave that part to the inspection.

From outside, during daylight, check whether any tree limbs touch or overhang the roofline near where you hear the noise. That single detail often points straight to how the animal is getting in.

What not to do.

Avoid these before the visit.

  • Do not set off-the-shelf poison yourself without knowing what is actually causing the noise.
  • Do not seal any gap you find yourself before an inspection; you could trap an animal inside.
  • Do not sweep or vacuum any droppings you find; wet them first or leave it for the technician.

What to tell us on the call.

Give us the location in the house, the time of night, the type of sound, and whether you noticed any tree limbs near the roofline. That handful of details usually gets a technician started in the right spot the moment they arrive, which means a faster, more accurate first visit.

From there, the normal process applies: inspection, then trapping and removal, then sealing the entry point so the noise does not come back once the current animal is gone.

What a technician does with your notes once on site.

The details you gather before calling are not just for our records, they actively shape where the inspection starts. A technician who knows the sound is heaviest over the garage, happens mainly after 10pm, and the home has an oak limb touching the roofline nearby will check that exact combination first rather than walking the entire attic blind.

That focused starting point often means a shorter, more accurate first visit, and it is the main reason we ask these specific questions on the call rather than just scheduling a generic inspection.

If the sound happens at roughly the same time every night, note that too. A consistent nightly pattern, versus random, scattered timing, can suggest an established routine rather than new or passing activity, which is a useful detail even though it will not change the inspection itself.

If more than one household member has heard the noise independently, compare notes on exactly what it sounded like and where. Two slightly different descriptions of the same event, one person hearing scratching, another hearing more of a thump, together usually give a fuller picture than either account alone.

If you have a baby monitor or similar device already placed somewhere in the house, it can sometimes pick up attic sounds more clearly than standing in a room listening directly, simply due to microphone placement and sensitivity.

Noting whether the sound happens on both calm and windy nights can also help rule out tree branches brushing the roof as the actual cause, since that kind of sound tends to track with wind rather than occurring consistently regardless of weather.

Whatever you notice, write it down before you forget it. A quick note on your phone the moment you hear something is more reliable than trying to remember the details a day or two later when you finally get around to calling.

What to do, and not do, while you wait

These steps follow CDC cleanup guidance for rodent droppings and nests. They are general safety practice for any household.

  • Skip the broom and the vacuum on dry droppings. Stirring them up is how dust gets airborne.
  • Open doors or vents and let an enclosed space air out for 30 minutes or more before cleanup.
  • Wear rubber or plastic gloves, and wash your hands well afterward, even if you wore gloves.
  • Spray the area with disinfectant, wait roughly five minutes, then wipe it up with paper towels instead of dry material.
  • Seal used paper towels and any dead rodent in a bag before disposal. Keep bare skin out of it.
  • If you develop fever, muscle aches or trouble breathing in the weeks after cleaning up rodent droppings, tell a doctor about the exposure. We are not medical professionals and cannot judge individual health risk.

Common questions

Is it normal to hear nothing during the day but activity at night?

Yes, that is the typical pattern for both mice and rats, which are mostly nocturnal. Daytime activity on top of that can suggest a larger population.

Could the noise be something other than a rodent?

Squirrels and birds can also get into attics and produce similar sounds. The inspection will confirm which animal is actually present before any treatment plan is suggested.

Is it safe to go into the attic myself to check?

Only if access is easy and safe, during the day, and only to look, not to touch droppings or disturb insulation. When in doubt, leave it to the inspection.

Does a consistent nightly pattern mean a bigger problem?

Not necessarily bigger, but it does suggest the animal has settled into a routine rather than just passing through, which the inspection will confirm either way.
