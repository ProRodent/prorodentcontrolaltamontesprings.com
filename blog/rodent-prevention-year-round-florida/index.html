A Year-Round Rodent Prevention Calendar for Central Florida Homes

Central Florida does not get a true off-season for rodents. Here is a simple, honest calendar of what to check and when.

Because Central Florida does not get the hard freeze that slows rodent activity further north, there is no true off-season here, which can make prevention feel less urgent at any one moment. A simple, honest seasonal checklist still helps, even without a winter lull to plan around.

Late winter and early spring (January to March).

This is typically the quietest stretch of the year here, though still far from inactive. It is a reasonable time to handle landscaping maintenance, trimming tree limbs back from the roofline and clearing dense ground cover from the foundation, before the busier warm months increase activity.

Spring into early summer (April to June).

Activity typically picks up as temperatures and humidity rise. This is a good window for a proactive inspection if you have not had one recently, checking garage door seals, roof vents and foundation gaps before the rainy season adds flooding-driven displacement into the mix.

Rainy season and storm season (June to October).

Florida's wettest months bring the highest risk of storm-driven displacement, when flooding along canals and retention ponds pushes ground-dwelling rodents toward higher, drier shelter, often a garage, shed or attic. See our guide on storm-driven rodent displacement for the full pattern.

  • Check garages and sheds for new activity after any significant storm.
  • Keep stored items in sealed bins rather than cardboard through this stretch.
  • Report new attic or garage activity promptly rather than waiting to see if it settles.

Fall (October to December).

As outdoor food sources shift and shelter becomes more valuable with slightly cooler nights, roof rats in particular tend to push harder toward attics and other indoor shelter. This is often the busiest stretch for attic-related calls in our experience, even without a hard freeze driving it the way it would further north.

The one constant: sealing holds up year-round.

Unlike trapping, which responds to active animals, sealing entry points pays off no matter what time of year it happens, since Central Florida's climate does not give rodents a true break at any point. If you are planning exclusion and entry point sealing work, there is no wrong season to do it.

A realistic approach.

How this calendar differs by part of our coverage area.

Towns with heavier tree canopy, Winter Park, Maitland and the older sections of Altamonte Springs itself, tend to see the fall attic-activity increase most sharply, given how much that pattern depends on roof rats using tree cover. Towns with more open or agricultural-adjacent land, like parts of Apopka, Plymouth and Zellwood, see a comparatively steadier pattern of ground-level activity across the warmer months rather than as sharp a fall spike.

Either way, the sealing work that actually stops repeat activity holds its value in every part of our coverage area, regardless of which seasonal pattern a particular property leans toward.

If you only have time or budget for one prevention step a year, sealing entry points generally outperforms a single seasonal trapping round, since it addresses the reason new animals keep arriving rather than just the ones currently present. Trapping still matters once activity is confirmed, but as a once-a-year investment, sealing holds up better across every season on this calendar.

None of this calendar replaces calling as soon as you notice an actual problem, regardless of the season. The seasonal patterns described here are useful for planning preventive steps, not a reason to wait on an active infestation until the "right" time of year comes around.

Keeping a simple written note of when you first notice any activity each year, even just a one-line log, can help identify whether your own property follows the general seasonal pattern described here or something specific to its own landscaping and construction.

What to do, season by season.

  • There is no off-season here, just quieter and busier stretches.
  • Landscaping and sealing work pays off whenever it happens.
  • Fall and post-storm periods deserve extra attention to attics and garages.

What to do, and not do, while you wait

These steps follow CDC cleanup guidance for rodent droppings and nests. They are general safety practice for any household.

  • Never dry sweep or vacuum droppings, urine stains or nesting material.
  • Ventilate a shut-up space first. Half an hour of airing out is the CDC's starting point.
  • Put on gloves before touching anything contaminated, then wash your hands thoroughly when you finish.
  • Wet droppings with a disinfectant or a bleach solution and let it soak for about five minutes before wiping with paper towels.
  • Bag the waste, seal the bag, and put it in the trash. Do not handle live or dead rodents with bare hands.
  • If you develop fever, muscle aches or trouble breathing in the weeks after cleaning up rodent droppings, tell a doctor about the exposure. We are not medical professionals and cannot judge individual health risk.

Common questions

Is there really no quiet season at all in Florida?

There is a relatively quieter stretch in late winter, but it is a difference of degree, not a true off-season the way a hard freeze creates further north.

Should I schedule a yearly inspection even without signs of a problem?

A proactive check, especially heading into the busier fall months, can catch small gaps before they become an active problem, though it is not strictly required if you have no signs of activity.

Does this calendar apply to both mice and roof rats?

The general seasonal pattern applies to both, though roof rats are more tied to tree canopy and attics specifically, while mice show more consistent activity tied to food and shelter access year round.

If I can only do one thing this year, what should it be?

Sealing entry points generally gives the most lasting value as a single yearly investment, since it addresses why new animals keep arriving rather than only removing the ones currently present.
