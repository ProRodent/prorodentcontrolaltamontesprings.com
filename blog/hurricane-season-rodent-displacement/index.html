Why Rodent Calls Pick Up After Florida's Heavy Summer Storms

Heavy summer rain and flooding push rodents toward higher, drier ground, often straight into a garage or attic. Here is what to watch for.

Altamonte Springs homeowners sometimes notice an uptick in rodent activity right after a heavy summer storm or a stretch of Florida's rainy season, and the pattern is not a coincidence. Flooding in low-lying yards, retention ponds and drainage canals displaces ground-dwelling rodents, and they move toward the nearest dry, sheltered spot, which is often a garage, shed or the base of a house itself.

What actually happens during heavy rain.

Burrows along canal banks and retention pond edges, common throughout neighborhoods like Country Creek that sit close to Seminole County's drainage network, flood during heavy rain, forcing the animals living in them to relocate. Roof rats, already comfortable climbers, often move upward toward rooflines and attics instead of staying at ground level once their usual cover is compromised.

This is separate from the baseline year-round activity Central Florida sees thanks to mild winters; storm-driven displacement is a shorter, sharper spike layered on top of that steady background level.

Where displaced rodents tend to end up.

  • Garages and sheds near canal banks or retention ponds.
  • Attics, via tree limbs or roofline gaps, once ground shelter floods.
  • Vehicles parked for extended periods near flooded or low-lying areas.

What to check after a storm.

If your property backs onto a canal, pond or other low-lying drainage area, a quick check after a heavy storm, from the outside, during daylight, for new droppings, rub marks or disturbed mulch along the fence line can catch a problem before it moves indoors.

Garages are worth a particular look if they have taken on water during a storm, since a garage that flooded even briefly can both attract displaced rodents and lose some of its usual deterrents, like dry stored food that got wet and needs disposal anyway.

What a post-storm visit covers.

A technician checks the usual entry points plus anything storm-related: new gaps from wind damage, displaced mulch or landscaping that opened a foundation gap, and any area that took on water. See backyard and outdoor rodent control for how yard-level work after flooding is typically scoped.

Preparing before the next storm.

What this looks like across different parts of our coverage area.

Properties near canals and retention ponds, common in neighborhoods like Country Creek, see the clearest version of this pattern, since the burrow habitat flooding directly is right at the edge of the yard. Towns further from water, like parts of Longwood or Apopka with more open or agricultural-adjacent land, see a related but slightly different version: general ground saturation pushing rodents toward any dry structure nearby, not necessarily a specific flooded burrow.

Either way, the response is the same: check garages, sheds and attics for new activity in the days following a significant storm, rather than assuming a quiet winter is the only time rodent risk changes.

Even without a direct hit from a named storm, a stretch of several days of heavy, sustained rain common during Florida's wet season can produce the same displacement effect on a smaller scale. It does not take a hurricane specifically to push ground-dwelling rodents toward higher, drier shelter near a home.

Clearing storm debris promptly after a major weather event also helps on the prevention side. Downed branches, loose yard waste and temporary debris piles left for days after a storm give displaced rodents additional cover right at the time they are already looking for new shelter.

Checking on elderly neighbors or relying on the help of a neighbor after a major storm is good practice generally, and it is also a reasonable moment to mention this pattern to them if their property backs onto the same canal or pond system.

Even a property without direct canal or pond frontage can see a version of this pattern. If a neighboring yard floods, the displaced activity can spread across a shared property line, which is part of why a whole-block perspective sometimes helps explain a sudden new problem.

Insurance considerations are worth a brief mention too. Storm-related rodent displacement is generally treated differently by insurers than storm damage itself, so check with your carrier about what, if anything, is covered if you end up needing repair work tied to this kind of post-storm activity.

What to do before and after a storm.

  • Keep stored items in sealed bins rather than cardboard, especially in garages prone to minor flooding.
  • Check and clear drainage away from the foundation after major storms.
  • Report any new activity promptly rather than waiting to see if it settles on its own.
  • Do not seal an exterior gap yourself until you know what is already on the other side of it; trapping an animal inside a wall can cause worse damage.

Before you call: safe steps for rodent signs

This is general public-health guidance from the CDC, not a description of any one job. It applies whether you call us or anyone else.

  • Never dry sweep or vacuum droppings, urine stains or nesting material.
  • Ventilate a shut-up space first. Half an hour of airing out is the CDC's starting point.
  • Gloves on first, handwashing last. Do both every time.
  • Soak the droppings and the surface in disinfectant first. Wiping comes only after the soak time.
  • Seal used paper towels and any dead rodent in a bag before disposal. Keep bare skin out of it.
  • Any fever, aches or breathing trouble after handling rodent waste should be checked by a doctor. We do not make health claims about individual exposure.

Common questions

Does every storm cause a rodent spike?

Not every storm, but heavier, more sustained rain events that actually flood low-lying burrow areas are the ones most likely to push activity toward homes.

Can I prevent this entirely?

Not entirely, since storm-driven displacement is a natural response to flooding, but sealing entry points in advance limits how much of that displaced activity actually makes it indoors.

Should I call right after a storm even without obvious signs yet?

If your property backs onto a pond or canal and took on significant water, an inspection soon after a major storm can catch early activity before it becomes a bigger indoor problem.

Does this only happen during named storms?

No, several days of sustained heavy rain during the normal wet season can cause the same displacement effect on a smaller scale, without any named storm involved.
